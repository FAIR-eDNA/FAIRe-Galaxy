2.1   |   Day/Night UVC and eDNA Surveys
Tandem underwater visual census (UVC) and environmental
DNA (eDNA) surveys were conducted at three coastal temperate
reef sites: Fortescue Bay, Tinderbox Marine Reserve, and Waubs
Bay, Bicheno in Tasmania, Australia, in the summer months of
November 2021, February and March 2022 (Figure 1a, Table S1
for coordinate information). Each site was surveyed using tandem eDNA/UVC methods every 6 h (four times within a 24-­h
period; time-­points: 10 am, 4 pm, 10 pm, and 4 am). The 10 am
and 4 pm time points were within daylight hours; the 10 pm
and 4 am time points were during the night (see Table S1 for
recorded sunrise and sunset times). The shallow rocky reef
surveyed in Fortescue Bay was characterized by large granite
boulders, dominated mostly by the macroalgae Ecklonia radiata, with a mix of understory species. The section of reef surveyed at the Tinderbox Marine Reserve was characterized by a
sandstone rocky foundation, dominated by E. radiata with a diverse composition of red, green, and brown macroalgae species

were made along a 50-­m transect line set at a 5-­m depth contour,
which was deployed at the commencement of the first sampling
event. The identity and abundance of the broader fish assemblage were quantified within 5 m belts either side of the transect
line (i.e., 500 m2 total survey area; Figure 1b), observations of
the cryptobenthic fish and macro-­invertebrate assemblage was
constrained to 1 m belts (i.e., 100 m2 total; Figure 1c) to assist in
detection of these more cryptic species. Upon completion, the
transect line was left in place, identified by a surface marker
float to assist with site relocation for night surveys. Surveys undertaken at night followed the same sampling protocol as day.
Detection of nocturnal species was facilitated by LED white
light dive torches (1300 lm, 120° beam, Big blue AL1300XWP).
Any species not identifiable in situ were photographed and identified postsurvey using appropriate field guides.

2.2   |   Laboratory Processing of eDNA

Five eDNA seawater samples were collected by divers adjacent
to the seafloor (depth of 5 m) at the start and end of the transects using bleach-­sterilized Nalgene bottles (total 10 × 1 L eDNA
samples per time point, per site). Seawater eDNA samples were
then individually filtered across 0.45 μm 47 mm MF-­Millipore
mixed cellulose ester (MCE) membranes using a Masterflex
L/S Variable-­Speed Digital Drive pump within 1 h of collection.
A 10% bleach solution was used to clean filtration equipment
between time points at a site, followed by a rinse in Milli-­Q
(MilliporeSigma) water. A one-­liter sample of the Milli-­Q rinse

DNA was extracted from half of each filter membrane within
2 months of collection using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue
Kit (Qiagen) with the following modifications: 540 μL of ATL
lysis buffer, 60 μL of Proteinase K and a 3-­h digest at 56°C.
Postdigestion, 300 μL of the digested solution was combined
with 300 μL of buffer AL and 300 μL of ethanol. An extraction
blank was processed in parallel with every set of 23 x 1 L samples
to detect any cross-­contamination. Two previously published
metabarcoding assays (16S Fish and COI Leray, see Table 1)
were employed to amplify fish (teleosts and elasmobranchs) and
invertebrates from mixed environmental samples.

were then quality filtered (minimum sequence length = 100 bp,

Taxonomic assignment of ASVs was performed using the lowest
common ancestor (LCA) Python script in eDNAFlow (Mousavi-­
Derazmahalleh et al. 2021). In this pipeline, BLAST matches required a threshold qCov of 96%, a minimum percentage identity
of 85%, and a difference (Diff) of 1. A species-­level assignment
required a minimum percentage identity of 98% with a qCov of
100%. In cases where an ASV had multiple top hits within 1%
similarity of each other, the assigned taxonomy was collapsed
back to the lowest common ancestor. ASVs were then merged
by taxonomy using the phyloseq (v1.24.2) “tax_glom” function
(McMurdie and Holmes 2013) in R. Individual replicates were
not subsampled to an equal read depth, as ASV accumulation
within individual samples plateaued with the high level of sequencing read depth afforded by the Illumina NovaSeq platform
(Figure S1; see ‘Section 3.1’).

2.3   |   Bioinformatic Processing of eDNA Sequences

To make the eDNA dataset comparable with the taxa being documented in the standardized UVC-­diver transect survey method,
we extracted fish (teleost and elasmobranch) and invertebrate
eDNA detections from both the 16S Fish and COI Leray datasets. These were consolidated into two taxonomic abundance
matrices (1. fish, and 2. macroinvertebrates). Two different semiquantitative metrics were used to summarize eDNA detections:
(1) presence-­absence data for each eDNA replicate per timepoint
(n = 10) per site was summed to provide a count of replicate detections (n/10 replicates) and (2) proportional read abundance
of each taxa across the same 10 replicates (n/1). While analyzed
independently, these two semiquantitative eDNA datasets have
a strong, positive correlation (Spearman rank correlation coefficient ρ = 0.762, p = 0.001). UVC taxonomic abundance matrices
with observational count data for 1. fishes and 2. macroinvertebrates were generated for comparative analyses with the
eDNA data.

an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (SP flow cell: 500 cycle,
2 × 250 bp) at AGRF.

Taxa accumulation by UVC survey and eDNA replicate per
site was plotted using the vegan (v2.6.4) “specaccum” function (Oksanen et al. 2019) in R. Observed taxonomic richness
of both fish and macroinvertebrates was tested for significant
variation between survey methods (UVC and eDNA), the three
sampling sites (Fortescue Bay, Tinderbox Marine Reserve, and
Waubs Bay, Bicheno), and time surveyed (day and night) using
a three-­way ANOVA and graphed using the ggplot2 package
(Wickham 2016) in R.
Community compositional variation in fishes and macroinvertebrates was analyzed on a combined UVC/eDNA dataset (converted to presence-­absence) and standalone quantitative UVC
(count) and semiquantitative eDNA (proportional read abundance [n/1] and count of replicate detections [n/10]) datasets.
The standalone datasets were analyzed separately to retain the
quantitative and semiquantitative metrics of the two methods,
which, when combined, were normalized to presence-­absence.
The combined UVC/eDNA presence-­absence matrix was converted to a Jaccard similarity matrix; the standalone UVC
count, eDNA proportional read abundance, and eDNA count of
replicate detection were converted to Bray–Curtis dissimilarity
matrices in PRIMER v7 (Clarke and Gorley 2015). These similarity matrices were then tested for compositional variation by a
distance-­based linear routine (DistLM) using a step-­w ise selection procedure and adjusted R 2 criterion in the PERMANOVA+
add-­on (Anderson, Gorley, and Clarke 2008) of PRIMER v7.
Predictor variables included survey method (UVC/eDNA), site
(Fortescue Bay, Tinderbox Marine Reserve, and Waubs Bay,
Bicheno), and time of sampling (day/night).
For both fish and macroinvertebrates, community composition was visualized by principal coordinate analysis (PCO) in
PRIMER v7. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between individual taxa and the PCO axes; correlation coefficients
> 0.5 and < −0.5 were regarded as indicating strong positive and
strong negative correlations, respectively. Taxa with a strong
correlation to the PCO1 and/or PCO2 ordination axes (representative of the top two predictor variables with the highest proportion of explained variance) were overlaid as vectors on the
PCO plots; line direction and length illustrating direction and
strength of taxa correlation, respectively. The detection profiles
of the top species driving dissimilarity between day and night in
the UVC dataset were plotted over the time intervals with count
data from UVC, eDNA proportional abundance (n/1) and eDNA
replicate detections (n/10). This indicated whether reef species
with strong diel activity, as observed by UVC, produced varying
levels of eDNA detectability across 24-­h or whether the diurnal/
nocturnal species maintained a basal level of eDNA detection,
irrespective of sampling time.

3   |   Results
3.1   |   eDNA Sequencing Statistics
The two metabarcoding assays applied across the 132 seawater
eDNA extracts and controls yielded a total of 113,830,822 sequencing reads. The mean number of quality filtered sequencing

reads per replicate sample (excluding filtration controls and
blanks) was 482,503 ± 258,374 (SD) for the 16S Fish assay and
283,728 ± 186,0.18 (SD) for the COI Leray assay (Table S2). No
sequencing reads from the filtration and extraction blanks
made it through bioinformatic processing. Human eDNA from
the divers during collection or sample processing was detected
in both the 16S and COI datasets, however at very low levels
(average read depth per sample was 0.0004% and 0.13%, respectively). Fish and macroinvertebrate detections were extracted
from the filtered datasets of the two assays (consistent with
macrofauna targeted by RLS methods only) and were then consolidated across the replicates (n = 10) from each time interval
per site (Table S3). Regarding the eDNA taxonomic assignments,
only 50/115 eDNA-­detected fish taxa and 23/47 eDNA-­detected
macroinvertebrate taxa could be confidently provided at a
species level (match identity > 98%, qCov 100%); the remaining taxa assignments were provided at a genus or family level.
Comparatively, nearly all UVC taxonomic assignments for both
fish (36/37) and macroinvertebrates (32/32), were provided at a
species level.

3.2   |   Observed Fish and Macroinvertebrate
Richness
The UVC method observed a total of 37 fish taxa (36 species-­
level, 1 genus-­level) and 32 macroinvertebrate taxa (all species-­
level) across the three combined Tasmanian temperate reef sites.
Of this, 27 fish taxa and 24 macroinvertebrate taxa were detected
solely by the UVC method, such as Dotalabrus aurantiacus,
Hippocampus abdominalis, Mugil cephalus, Plagusia chabrus,
Macroctopus maorum, and Turbo undulatus. Comparatively, the
eDNA method detected a total of 115 fish taxa (50 species-­level,
56 genus-­level, and 9 family-­level) and 47 macroinvertebrate
taxa (23 species-­level, 16 genus-­level, and 8 family-­level). Of
this, 105 fish taxa and 39 macroinvertebrate taxa were detected
solely by the eDNA method. It should be noted that comparison of species-­level detections between methods is hampered to
some degree due to the many eDNA taxonomic assignments at a
genus and family level only. For example, Conger detected in the
eDNA dataset probably corresponds to Conger verreauxi as it is
the only regional species in that genus. Despite this limitation,
eDNA did overall detect more fish species (n = 50) than UVC;
this included species unobserved by UVC, such as Lotella rhacina, Galeorhinus galeus, Girella tricuspidata, and Chironemus
georgianus. The eDNA method did not detect as many macroinvertebrate species (n = 23) as UVC; however, it detected
unique species not reported by UVC such as Cenolia tasmaniae,
Meridiastra calcar, and the invasive sea star Asterias amurensis.
